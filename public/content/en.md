# Chapter One | The Orphan Without Parents

## 01 | An Old Legend: The Child Bound to a Chair

> **Source notice:** Folklore / literary reconstruction | Versions differ; this is not presented as established history

### Classical inscription and present-day reflection | An old legend

> **Care for the young in your own family, and extend that care to the young of others.**

To cherish one’s own child is human feeling. The true measure of a society is whether it can extend the same protection to children with no ties of blood, no public voice, and no power to seek help.

> **“Children have ears, but no mouth.” — Taiwanese saying**  
> A child may hear everything and still be unable to explain the pain. Adults must not mistake quietness for safety.

### Complete shadow-play dialogue

> **Literary shadow-play narration | Not dialogue spoken by people in the case | Not a real recording | Not a court finding**

- `S01-L01` **Woman:** The window paper has yellowed, the plum branch has withered, and a new generation now tells the tale. Why does this chair remain where it was, as though waiting for hands that should have arrived long ago?

- `S01-L02` **Man:** The legend says she suffered, and those who came later entrusted her suffering to ghosts and gods—as though, while the incense still burned, the living need not admit that a door once failed to open.

- `S01-L03` **Woman:** Do not turn a child’s cry into a curiosity. If she called out, the answer should not have come from a deity in the underworld, but from those beneath the same roof who could hear her.

- `S01-L04` **Man:** I have brought many versions. Every page tells the story differently. Only the empty chair remains unchanged in them all.

- `S01-L05` **Woman:** Then let the story stop beside the chair. Add not a single drop of blood to the legend. Leave only the question it can truly ask: when a child was trapped, did the adults see?

- `S01-L06` **Man:** I lower the scroll. From this moment, I will no longer ask how she became a legend. I will ask how the next child might never have to become one.

### Full text

*An old house outside time.*

There is no wind inside.

The window paper is yellow, like a letter left unopened for so long that no one now dares to break the seal. A dim lamp hangs from the wall. Each time the flame contracts, the wooden chair’s shadow grows another inch across the floor.

A child sits in the chair.

Her feet do not reach the ground. Her arms cannot fall freely at her sides. The knots are hidden among sleeves and shadow, like a sentence the adults have deliberately passed over. She does not cry—perhaps she has already cried. She does not call out—perhaps she called for too long. A child’s voice is slight to begin with. In a room crowded with adult explanations, it becomes as light as ash.

Many versions of the Chair Girl legend—*Í-á-koo*, 椅仔姑—circulate in Taiwanese folklore.

Some say she was a girl who died after enduring great cruelty. Some say she was confined to a chair and later became a spirit whom people could summon with questions. As the story traveled, its details were patched and repatched like old cloth. Truth and invention became entangled, leaving one image that no one can verify and no one can quite forget: a child unable to escape, and adults beside her who did not loosen the bonds in time.

Generations later, people still repeat her name, as though remembrance might keep the child who was not saved from disappearing entirely.

But a legend cannot testify for reality, and it must not become the answer to any real case.

It leaves only a few questions—questions that feel painfully modern and remain difficult to answer:

When a child is trapped and loses her voice, do the adults nearby see her?

When faint warnings appear again and again, will anyone pause and move closer?

When everyone says they are responsible for only one small part, who is responsible for bringing the whole child safely into tomorrow?

*The chair fills the frame.*

Its rough legs fade into the dark. The grain of the wood becomes pale scratches on a hospital floor. The shadow of a cord lengthens into a hanging IV tube. The dim wall lamp flares into the fluorescent light of an emergency department that never goes dark.

The sound of a gurney comes from far away.

The old house withdraws into darkness. A door in another age is being pushed open in haste.

## 02 | When a Child Must Leave Home

> **Source notice:** Explanation of systems | Adoption placement, out-of-home placement, and round-the-clock care must be read as distinct arrangements

### Classical inscription and present-day reflection | Leaving home

> **Who says the heart of a blade of grass can repay the warmth of three spring suns?**

A child’s need for attachment and care does not disappear when a family encounters hardship. When the family of origin cannot carry that responsibility for a time, the system does not take charge of a file. It takes charge of a childhood still being formed.

> **“Each blade of grass has its drop of dew.” — Taiwanese saying**  
> Every life needs care that truly belongs to it. A referral is not the transfer of responsibility; it is the duty to make sure the next pair of hands has taken hold.

### Complete shadow-play dialogue

> **Literary shadow-play narration | Not dialogue spoken by people in the case | Not a real recording | Not a court finding**

- `S02-L01` **Woman:** When a child leaves a familiar embrace, he carries very little: one set of clothes, a pair of shoes, and a trust in adults that has not yet learned suspicion.

- `S02-L02` **Man:** The first door says the case has been accepted. The second says a referral is under way. The third says an assessment is pending. Every sentence contains a procedure, and every sentence points to another door.

- `S02-L03` **Woman:** Before you passed on the ribbon, did you see clearly the hands that would receive it? To hand over is not to set down. A referral does not end one’s responsibility.

- `S02-L04` **Man:** I thought that if every door were connected, the road could not break. Only now do I see that the space between one door and the next is precisely where a child can fall most easily.

- `S02-L05` **Woman:** He cannot choose who carries him away or ask where he is being sent. The adults who do have choices must do more than deliver the form.

- `S02-L06` **Man:** Then let this ribbon pass through every door without leaving our hands. At every handover, someone must look back and ask: did the child truly arrive in safety?

### Full text

*One pair of hands passes a child to another.*

The adult world is very good at giving names to separation.

Referral. Assessment. Matching. Placement. Handover. Visit. Each word appears neatly on a form, in the same type size, without tears or warmth. To a very young child, however, they may mean only this: the person who held him yesterday is gone today.

In Taiwan, some children enter the adoption process because their families of origin can no longer provide care. Others are temporarily placed with relatives, foster families, group homes, or residential institutions because of safety, care, or protection needs. This is known as out-of-home placement.

Adoption and out-of-home placement are not the same.

Adoption creates a new legal parent-child relationship recognized by a court. Out-of-home placement provides a temporary care setting when a child cannot safely remain with the family of origin. Round-the-clock childcare while a child awaits adoption may occupy yet another legal and administrative position; it is not necessarily the same as formal out-of-home placement, either in legal status or in the public authorities and oversight mechanisms involved. After Kaikai’s death, this gray area became one of the central subjects of public debate.

These classifications must remain clear because responsibility cannot be sustained through ambiguity. From the child’s physical point of view, however, all of these arrangements begin at the same place:

He must leave the people he knows and be placed in another pair of hands.

The purpose of the system is to provide safer, more stable care. Yet every handover also creates another seam in the duty to protect.

Who confirms that the child is actually receiving proper care?

Who sees the place where he is living with their own eyes, rather than seeing only a photograph sent to a phone?

Who notices injuries, a sudden change in behavior, unusual weight, or a silence that cannot be explained?

If a caregiver repeatedly postpones a meeting, who goes to the door before the next appointment?

A child must not slowly disappear among administrative boundaries, divided institutional duties, visit forms, and a caregiver’s explanations.

Nor should waiting for a new legal family cost him the one place to which he might truly return.

## 03 | When More Calls for Help Enter the System

> **Source notice:** Government statistics | Figures use the latest complete year directly verifiable in the official statistical database

### Classical inscription and present-day reflection | More calls for help

> **See the smallest sign and know what is beginning; see the first thread and know where it may end.**

Danger rarely appears for the first time on the final day. The value of professional judgment lies in gathering scattered signs and recognizing the risk that is drawing near.

> **“It is not enough to have seen; one must see clearly.” — Taiwanese saying**  
> Having looked is a completed step in a procedure. Understanding what was seen is what may become protection.

### Complete shadow-play dialogue

> **Literary shadow-play narration | Not dialogue spoken by people in the case | Not a real recording | Not a court finding**

- `S03-L01` **Man:** Tonight, many windows light again, and many calls are entered into the record. The figures stand in such orderly rows that disorder itself appears, at last, to have a boundary.

- `S03-L02` **Woman:** A number does not wake in terror at night, nor hide behind a door from fear. You are counting reports, but behind those entries may stand the same child, asking for help again and again.

- `S03-L03` **Man:** I can prove that someone knocked, but this table alone cannot prove the door was opened. I can count who was seen, but not who was truly brought to safety.

- `S03-L04` **Woman:** Then do not let the figures become a wall. Lower the statistics and look at the bed, the schoolbag, the bowl behind them—and at how a child waits through each day.

- `S03-L05` **Man:** I once believed that the more data we had, the closer truth would come. Now the long table is buried beneath paper, and the place where the child should be is empty.

- `S03-L06` **Woman:** The blank space does not mean there is no information. It reminds you that the papers were never blank. What went missing was the child beyond the papers.

### Full text

*A table of statistics fills the page.*

The paper is white. The figures are black.

Line after line descends in silence, almost without feeling. Figures do not cry or wake in the night. They prove only how many calls for help reached the threshold of the system.

As of the fact-checking date for this edition, the latest complete year that can be verified directly in the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s child statistics portal is 2024. In that year, 62,314 child-protection investigations involved 59,559 children. The number of cases is not the number of children found to have been abused, and one child may be involved in more than one report or investigation. These figures describe the volume received by the system; they are not evidence of causation in any individual case.

Being seen, however, has never been the same as being saved.

- **59,559 children** | Children involved in those investigations; the unit differs from the case count.

- **57,090 children** | Children provided with follow-up services after investigation and assessment.

- **10,810 children** | Children classified within those services as receiving protective services. The remainder received welfare services or referrals to other resources. This figure must not be treated as the number found by a court to have been abused.

This project accessed it on August 16, 2026.

*The view settles on the unit printed after each figure.*

Behind “cases” may be the same child calling for help more than once. Behind “children” is a bed, a schoolbag, and a set of clothes carried into an unfamiliar room. To an administrative system, they are annual figures. To a child, they are an entire childhood.

Some children reach a safe home. Some begin to trust adults again with foster families, relatives, or residential services. Others remain beyond rescue even after the system has learned their names.

A report is a knock at the door.

The difficult question is what happens after the knock: does someone open it in time?

## 04 | An Emergency-Room Memory from Behind the Curtain

> **Source notice:** Anonymous reader’s letter / reconstructed scene | Not a medical record, not a recording, and not a court finding

### Classical inscription and present-day reflection | A memory from behind the curtain

> **In this life we so often fail to meet, like Shen and Shang—stars that never share the sky.**

Some partings are not journeys into the distance. They take place across a hospital curtain or an emergency-room door, leaving two people forever on opposite sides of a divide that can no longer be crossed.

> **“Even a small child’s body can speak for itself.” — Taiwanese saying**  
> A very young child may be unable to explain what happened. The body, nutritional state, reactions, and injuries may still leave signs that require professional recognition.

### Complete shadow-play dialogue

> **Literary shadow-play narration | Not dialogue spoken by people in the case | Not a real recording | Not a court finding**

- `S04-L01` **Woman:** Listen—the wheels cross the seams between the floor tiles, and every footstep runs toward the same door. The hospital has finally seen the danger, but this is already the last night of the child’s life.

- `S04-L02` **Man:** I want to draw the curtain and see what happened beyond it. Yet to witness is not to intrude, and sorrow should not be performed again merely to satisfy our curiosity.

- `S04-L03` **Woman:** Then remain outside the curtain. Hear the medical team hurry, hear instruments touch lightly, and hear the sigh that is kept low but cannot be wholly contained.

- `S04-L04` **Man:** The child could not tell a complete story, but the changes in his body were never silent. Injury, weight loss, and withdrawal were already speaking for him.

- `S04-L05` **Woman:** At last, every adult began to run. But before this night, who went one step farther because something seemed wrong? Who wrote “we will look another day” and placed it in tomorrow?

- `S04-L06` **Man:** The machines can record only the values of this moment. What belongs in the record is why, before the final night, no one began running sooner.

## 05 | It Began with an Amendment: The Founding of the Child Welfare League Foundation

> **Source notice:** Law and institutional history | Historical statements and editorial questions are kept distinct

### Classical inscription and present-day reflection | It began with an amendment

> **Good intentions alone cannot govern; laws cannot put themselves into effect.**

Ideals and laws must take form in daily practice. However complete a system appears on paper, goodwill cannot walk to a child’s side unless someone goes through the door and confirms that the child is safe.

> **“Good intentions still need sound ways of acting.” — Taiwanese saying**  
> Goodwill needs method, oversight, and follow-through if it is not to lose the person it was meant to protect somewhere inside a complicated process.

- `S05-L01` **Man:** An amendment emerged from public debate and the wording of law, and an organization was born of many people’s advocacy. The type descended, and at last the rights owed to children appeared upon the page.

### Full text

*The white emergency-room curtain becomes an aging case file.*

The story moves back to the early 1990s.

The documents carry the gray cast of a photocopier, and their edges curl slightly. The internet has not yet become part of public life. “Children’s rights” is not yet a phrase spoken as a matter of course at every policy meeting.

According to CWLF’s public organizational history, legislators, professionals, and civic groups formed a committee at the end of 1990 to promote amendments to child-welfare law. In December 1991, the Child Welfare League Foundation was established. This feature uses only the years and organizational changes that can be checked directly on that history page; it does not repeat a meeting count for which no public primary citation has been located.

The organization began in law reform and advocacy. Its work later expanded into adoption services, searches for missing children, support for families in hardship, family reunification and restoration, childcare consultation, and other direct services.

No nonprofit writes a tragedy into its own history at the moment of its birth.

Such organizations begin with a benevolent belief: that professional practice can support what a family cannot; that a system can be more reliable than chance; and that forms, procedures, and trained people can shelter a child from the harshest winds of circumstance.

But the longer an organization lives, and the better known its name becomes, the more it must carry—not only the ideals of its founding, but the result of every service as it actually reaches a child.

Ideals appear in an institutional history. Responsibility takes place behind a door.

It takes place when a social worker does or does not see a child in person; when a supervisor does or does not question an anomaly; when an organization allows “we will visit another day” to become another postponement; and when public authorities do or do not exchange information before a child loses safety.

More than three decades is a long time—long enough for a law-reform group to become a major child-welfare organization, and long enough for society to ask after successive cases whether the “better world” once promised has reached every room where a child is living.

The file turns to 2019.

One sheet of paper remains open.

It bears another child’s name.

Peipei.

### Full text

*Hospital corridor, Lunar New Year 2019.*

The hospital is quieter during the holiday.

Firecrackers sound beyond the windows. Through the thick glass, they arrive only as a few dull knocks, like someone at a distant door. The red New Year couplet in the corridor is new. The characters for “peace and safety” are placed neatly upon the wall. The words are festive; the light in the hospital room remains cold and white.

According to the family’s later public account, Peipei was nine months old when her family applied to the Hsinchu County Government to place her for adoption, after which CWLF handled the process. Following more than two months in a caregiver’s round-the-clock care, her family received a call on the second day of the Lunar New Year in 2019 telling them that she was undergoing emergency treatment at a hospital.

Peipei was therefore not “a five-month-old infant.” The description more firmly supported by the public material now available is that her family applied for adoption placement when she was nine months old, and that she died after later spending more than two months in round-the-clock care. Existing public information does not support a more precise date for the interval between the application and the start of that care, so this project draws no further inference. What can be said is that she was still a very young child, far too young to understand what adoption meant.

*A public question, seven years later.*

In February 2026, a public post lifted Peipei’s name out of old news reports once again.

The author said that CWLF had previously left a clarification on her page and had also described the Kaikai case as unprecedented. She asked: if a child in Hsinchu had died in 2019 while receiving round-the-clock care arranged through CWLF as she awaited adoption, why was Peipei absent from the organization’s public explanation of whether anything comparable had happened before?

The post relayed an account attributed to Peipei’s aunt. According to that account, the family had been told that they would have to wait at least three to four months before they could visit. They never reached that day. On the second day of the Lunar New Year, a call came instead: Peipei had been taken to a hospital in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, or OHCA.

The family’s account in the post referred to what they understood about her medical condition, including intestinal torsion with necrosis, a respiratory infection, and what the family described as intracranial bleeding. The post also relayed the caregiver’s reported account: that nothing appeared wrong after a meal, and that when she later checked, the child’s body was already cold. Regarding the intracranial bleeding alleged by the family, the caregiver was reported to have said that the child might have fallen accidentally during a diaper change. The family questioned why no such event appeared in the care log the caregiver was required to complete.

The same post said that the family later filed a criminal complaint and that prosecutors decided not to bring charges. It also said that the family later reached a private settlement with CWLF. According to the post, the family wanted more than an apology. They wanted stronger oversight of caregivers, including consideration of video monitoring and more frequent visits by social workers. The post said CWLF responded that these matters concerned internal reform and that it could not promise a direction or timetable, but would take the proposals back for discussion with supervisors.

These details come from a public post and its retelling of the family’s account. This site has not obtained the complete non-prosecution disposition, care log, or settlement documents. Details that cannot be independently verified are therefore not presented as facts established in a judicial proceeding.

The deaths of Peipei and Kaikai must not be written as the same kind of death.

In a public statement issued in 2026, CWLF said that prosecutors had considered medical, emergency-response, postmortem, and forensic materials and concluded that Peipei died of intestinal torsion and pneumonia caused by a viral infection, not from human action. It said the caregiver was not prosecuted, and that the version of the non-prosecution disposition CWLF had seen contained no reference to the intracranial bleeding alleged by the family. CWLF also said that after the event it changed its caregiver contracts so that the person placing a child for adoption would be a party to the agreement, and advocated returning children awaiting adoption to the government placement system when out-of-home placement was needed.

CWLF’s public account of the prosecutorial disposition and judicial outcome must be identified for what it is: the organization’s statement.

To say so clearly is not to erase the family’s pain. In the same way, preserving the family’s questions does not permit this site to rewrite an acute medical death as abuse already established by law.

On one side is the non-prosecution disposition as quoted by the organization, together with the organization’s later explanation.

On the other is the call a family received during the New Year holiday, and the question an aunt still refused to set down years later.

Both sides have documents. The child is absent from both.

Whether Peipei is considered a “precedent” therefore depends on the question being asked.

If “precedent” means only the deliberate killing of a child by a caregiver as established through a judicial process, Peipei’s case and Kaikai’s are plainly different. If the question is whether a child awaiting adoption had previously died while in round-the-clock care arranged through CWLF, and whether that death left changes capable of protecting the next child, then Peipei should not be omitted from the system’s memory.

Peipei did not become a name remembered across Taiwan for years. Her story was like a thin page caught inside a thick file—drawn out once, then covered again. Only after another child died nearly five years later at the boundary between adoption arrangements and care did people remember her anew.

More than seven years have passed. Had Peipei lived, she would likely be carrying a schoolbag into an elementary-school classroom. Nearly three years have passed since Kaikai’s death. Had he lived, he too would likely be in preschool, learning to write his name.

Children should not repeatedly spend the entirety of their lives waiting while adults investigate, debate reform, and divide responsibility.

Different causes of death and different legal findings illuminate the same institutional question:

When a child has left the family of origin but has not yet reached a permanent new home, who bears an unbroken duty of care through the waiting in between?

### Full text

The monitor continues to sound.

The hospital room of 2019 slowly darkens. The next page is a record of a visit made in 2023.

Kaikai was born in February 2022. His mother sought help because she was unable to support him. The child entered an initial adoption service, but guardianship and related procedural issues prevented it from continuing. His maternal grandmother later obtained guardianship. In 2023, a local social welfare center referred the family to CWLF for a second adoption process.

On September 1, 2023, Kaikai was placed in the care of a round-the-clock caregiver working with CWLF.

From that day forward, the system did not lack knowledge of where he was.

His name was on a roster. His photographs appeared in messages. He had a social worker and childcare visits. A process appeared to be moving.

At the first visit on September 25, the record noted bruising on his forehead. The caregiver said he had struck it while playing.

At the second visit on October 23, the record described him as quieter and lacking energy. The caregiver said he had just awakened.

In November, the caregiver reported that three of his teeth had fallen out. A later park visit and messages concerning medical care introduced accounts involving teeth grinding, possible stress, and other physical conditions.

A fourth visit scheduled for December 5 did not take place. The caregiver said other children in the home were ill and asked the social worker to return another day. Information about Kaikai was subsequently provided by phone and message. In the early hours of December 24, he was taken to a hospital.

The door closed once more.

This time, a physician said there were no signs of life.

The bruise on his forehead.

The eyes without energy.

The missing teeth.

The visit that did not take place.

The photographs sent from one phone to another.

One accepted explanation settles over another, like too many quilts in winter. The adults believe the child is asleep. In truth, he is disappearing, little by little, from everyone’s field of vision.

In 2024, the case entered public view and shocked Taiwan. Only then did the wider public learn that a child not yet two years old had spent the final 115 days of his life at the boundary between adoption placement and round-the-clock care.

After Kaikai’s death, the system began to close gaps. Local governments became involved earlier in assessing whether adoption placement was necessary. When a child awaiting adoption required out-of-home placement, local authorities were to provide assistance under the law. For round-the-clock and overnight care, and for children under three, guidance emphasized unannounced visits, visits at different times of day, and seeing the child in person. It also called for clearer recognition of unexplained injuries, caregivers who avoided visits, and other indicators of risk.

Reform is necessary. Yet reform always appears most complete after a tragedy.

A system does not resemble the villain in a tale. It has no single face to identify and does not smile coldly for a camera. It becomes visible only as one handover remains unclear, one visit does not pass through the door, one abnormal sign is explained away, and one responsibility is passed to the next office.

Peipei and Kaikai had different causes of death and different legal findings. One cannot stand in for the other, and they must not be casually described as the same kind of case.

They do, however, leave behind one question that cannot be filed away under a category:

Once a child has left home, is there anyone among the state, the organization, and the entrusted caregiver who must confirm the child’s safety continuously, from the first day to the last?

The child in the old legend was held to a chair by cords.

Children in the real world can be held by things that are harder to see: procedure, divided authority, trust, and adults’ willingness to accept another adult’s explanation.

The chair recedes into the dark.

The old legend stops here.

The legend ends. The record begins.

What follows is no ghost story.

*Taipei District Court, May 2025.*

The wood in the courtroom is dark.

Air-conditioning descends from above and passes over rows of people sitting upright. The clock on the wall moves neither quickly nor slowly. Like every clock untouched by grief, it keeps perfect time.

Kaikai’s maternal grandmother is called to testify.

Before she is fully seated, tears begin to fall.

At first, she can answer only through sobs. The judge asks a question; she replies, her voice breaking between words. She says that after Kaikai was born, she cared for him for nearly two months. Because the family could not continue to support him, she sought assistance from the local social welfare authority. Later, after he was placed with a caregiver, she did not dare to visit often: every visit made it harder to part from him again.

She trusted that the social worker would see him on her behalf.

The sentence falls lightly in the courtroom, yet weighs more than any accusation.
